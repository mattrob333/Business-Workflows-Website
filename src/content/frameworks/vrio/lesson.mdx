## What it is

VRIO is four questions asked of one capability, in order, where the order is the argument.

*Valuable* — does it let the business exploit an opportunity or blunt a threat? *Rare* — do
few competitors have it? *Inimitable* — is it costly or slow for them to copy or substitute?
*Organised* — is the business actually arranged to capture the value it produces?

A capability that fails a gate stops there, and where it stops is the finding. Fail the first
two and you have parity: necessary, undistinguishing, most of what any company owns. Fail
imitability and you have a temporary advantage with a clock on it. Fail organisation and you
have the most frustrating result in the model — a real advantage the business cannot collect.
Pass all four and you have something worth building strategy on.

The gates are a filter, not a scorecard. Averaging them defeats the whole design: a capability
that is spectacularly valuable and not remotely rare is still parity, and no amount of scoring
enthusiasm on the first gate rescues it.

## When to run it, and when not

Run it after the canvas has listed key resources and key activities, so the candidates are the
things the business model actually leans on rather than the things that came to mind in the
room. Run it before any direction framework, because "which markets could we enter" is
unanswerable until somebody has said out loud what we have that travels.

Run it on capabilities, not assets. A fleet of vans is an asset; the ability to finish a
retrofit in an occupied building without a second visit is a capability. Assets are usually
purchasable, which is the same as saying they usually fail the second gate.

Do not run it on aspirations. A capability you are building is a capability gap, and gaps are a
separate output with a separate purpose. Do not run it to justify an investment already made —
the model has no defence against a team that has decided the answer, because every gate is a
judgement and every judgement can be argued upward. And do not run it on everything you own.
Four to six candidates is a diagnosis; twenty is an inventory nobody will read twice.

## How it became wallpaper

The capabilities slide flatters everything on it.

It is the version of VRIO that survives contact with a leadership meeting: a table, one row per
capability, four columns of ticks, and a bottom row that says *sustained advantage* about
almost everything. Nobody set out to lie. Each tick was defended sincerely in the moment it was
asked about, and each defence was locally reasonable.

Rarity is where it goes wrong first, because rarity is the only gate that cannot be answered
from inside the building. Answering it honestly means knowing what three named competitors can
do, and that is real work — interviews, lost-deal reasons, a look at who is hiring for what. So
the question quietly changes from *do few competitors have this* to *are we good at this*, and
the answer to that is almost always yes, which is why it feels fine.

Organisation goes wrong second, and more expensively. It is scored as a statement about
competence — we are well run, tick — when it is really a question about wiring: does the
incentive structure, the scheduling system and the reporting line let this advantage turn into
money? A company can be full of capable people and still be organised in a way that spills
value on the floor.

What is left is a table where every capability passes, which means the model has been run and
has separated nothing.

> A VRIO in which everything passes is not an assessment. It is a compliment with a table around it.

## What good looks like

**Rarity names a comparator.** Rare compared to whom, and how do we know? A named competitor and
a source beats a confident adjective every time, and the sources are ordinary: job postings,
lost-deal reasons, what a customer says when they are explaining why they used somebody else.

**Failures are findings.** Parity is not an insult. Most of a functioning business is parity by
design, and discovering that a beloved capability is table stakes is worth more than another
tick, because it stops the next planning cycle from resting on it.

**The organised gate is asked about the organisation.** Its failures are the most actionable
things this framework produces: an advantage that exists and cannot be captured is usually one
incentive, one approval route or one scheduling rule away from being captured, and that is a
cheaper fix than anything on the other three gates.

**Every verdict carries its evidence and its date.** A capability that was rare three years ago
may not be rare now, and the only way to notice is for the assessment to be re-runnable rather
than remembered.

**The sustained one gets the next question.** Naming the durable advantage is where most VRIO
runs stop, and it is half the work. The other half is asking whether the business can deliver
more of it — because a genuine advantage that the operation cannot scale is not only an
advantage. It is also, very often, the exact place the whole system runs out of room.
