## What it is

Two engines on one page, and the second one is the reason the first is worth running.

SWOT collects. It sorts what is true about a business into four quadrants along two axes —
internal or external, helpful or harmful — and produces strengths, weaknesses, opportunities
and threats. It is an inventory, and inventories are useful in the way that a stocked pantry
is useful.

TOWS generates. It takes the same four lists and crosses them: a strength against an
opportunity, a strength against a threat, a weakness against an opportunity, a weakness
against a threat. Each crossing that survives contact with reality mints a strategic option
with a shape — press the advantage, defend with what you have, fix in order to compete,
contain the damage. The option is the output. The lists were only ever the ingredients.

The two live on one page here because separating them is precisely how the framework fails.
A SWOT that is never crossed is a photograph of a pantry.

## When to run it, and when not

Run it late rather than early. Strengths should arrive from a capability assessment that has
already failed some of them, threats and opportunities from a terrain analysis with dates and
sources attached. Run it that way and the wall is a summary of work that has been done. Run it
first, as the opening exercise of a strategy day, and it becomes a collection of impressions
that everything downstream then has to treat as evidence.

Run it when a direction question is genuinely open — a new segment, a repositioning, a
defensive move — because those are the questions the cross is built to generate answers for.

Do not run it to produce four lists. That is the entire failure mode and it is worth stating
as a rule rather than a warning. Do not put an item in a quadrant because of who said it; the
same fact can be a strength and a weakness depending on which way you look at it, and that is a
finding to record, not an argument to settle by seniority. And do not run it on a business
nobody has measured, because a quadrant of adjectives sorts into a quadrant of adjectives.

## How it became wallpaper

Everyone has done the wall.

Four flip-charts taped up, a stack of sticky notes, forty minutes, and a facilitator saying
there are no wrong answers. Everyone writes. The notes go up. They get grouped. Somebody
photographs the wall, the photograph goes into the deck, and the session is judged a success
because the room participated and the quadrants filled.

Nothing was generated. Not one strategic option came off that wall, and none was expected to,
because the exercise was designed to end when the quadrants were full. Collection *felt* like
work — it was social, it was fast, everybody contributed — and the feeling of productivity
arrived at exactly the moment the framework was supposed to start.

The contents make it worse. Entries are adjectives with no source: *great team*, *strong
brand*, *supply chain issues*. The team may well be great. But an entry that cites nothing
cannot be argued with, cannot be re-checked in six months and cannot be crossed with anything —
because crossing "great team" with "market growing" generates "leverage our great team in the
growing market," which is a sentence and not a plan.

And then the wall stays up. Long after the revenue mix moved, the strengths are still the
strengths, because nothing on the wall was ever able to expire.

> Four lists are not a strategy. They are the ingredients, photographed.

## What good looks like

**Every entry cites something and says how sure it is.** A strength is a claim with evidence
behind it and a status on it — fact, supported inference, assumption. Entries nobody can source
are not thrown away; they become open questions, which is an honest output and a much better
one than a promotion to fact by silence.

**Entries are specific enough to cross.** "Crews that finish complex retrofits in occupied
buildings" crosses with something. "Operational excellence" does not. The test is simple: if
you cannot imagine the sentence this entry would produce when paired with a threat, it is not
finished.

**The same fact is allowed to appear twice.** A capability that wins work and cannot be scaled
is a strength and a weakness, and recording both is more useful than choosing.

**The cross is actually run.** All four kinds, deliberately: SO, ST, WO, WT. Most teams stop
after the comfortable diagonal, which is how strategy ends up being a list of things to press
harder on.

**Empty pairings are information.** Sixteen pairings will not produce sixteen options, and the
ones that produce nothing tell you where the business has no move — which is worth knowing
before somebody promises one.

**The options get ranked against the constraint, not against enthusiasm.** Generated options
are candidates. Some of them add work to the exact part of the business that has no room left,
and they will look excellent until somebody checks. That check is the next framework, and it is
the only thing standing between a good workshop and a busier year with the same output.
