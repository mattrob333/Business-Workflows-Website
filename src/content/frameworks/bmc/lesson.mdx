## What it is

The Business Model Canvas is nine boxes holding one sentence: who this business serves,
what it promises them, how the promise reaches them, what it takes to keep, and what comes
back. Segments, value propositions, channels, relationships and revenue streams above the
line; resources, activities, partners and cost structure underneath it.

It is a model in the engineering sense, not a picture in the marketing sense. The value is
not that the nine boxes look tidy on a wall. It is that nine loosely held opinions — usually
held by nine different people, at nine different levels of detail — get written down in one
place, in one grain, where they are finally able to disagree with each other.

That last part is the framework. A canvas that agrees with itself everywhere has not been
filled in honestly. Somewhere in a real business, the block describing what you promise and
the block describing what you have are in tension, and the canvas is the cheapest way to
make that tension legible before it becomes a schedule.

## When to run it, and when not

Run it first. Almost everything downstream needs to know what the business *is* before it
can say anything useful about it: the terrain frameworks need segments and partners, the
advantage frameworks need resources and activities, and the constraint cannot be located in
a system nobody has described. A canvas is four hours of work that saves four weeks of
arguing at cross purposes.

Run it again whenever something structural moves — a new segment, a different delivery
model, a pricing change, an acquisition. Not on a calendar. On an event.

Do not run it to decide something. The canvas has no opinion about what you should do next;
it will happily describe a business that is about to fail. Do not run it as a workshop
warm-up, where the goal is participation rather than accuracy. And do not run it on a
business you cannot get numbers for. Without figures the blocks fill with adjectives, and a
canvas of adjectives is indistinguishable from a canvas of facts at a distance of two metres
— which is exactly the distance most people read it from.

## How it became wallpaper

Everyone has seen the poster. Nine boxes printed at A0, a room of people with sticky notes,
forty minutes, a photograph, and then the photograph in a shared drive folder until someone
tidies up the folder.

The sticky notes are not the problem. The problem is that the canvas was treated as an
output. Something was produced, the session ended, and nothing downstream ever read the
result — because nothing *could* read it. A photograph has no fields. You cannot ask it
which segment carries the concentration risk, and it cannot tell you that it stopped being
true in March, because a picture of a business model goes stale silently. That is why the
poster is still on the wall two years after the revenue mix changed. Nobody lied. Nobody
looked either.

> A canvas nobody reads from is a photograph of a business. It stops being true quietly, and nothing tells you when.

## What good looks like

**Every block cites something.** A share of revenue comes from the accounting export, a
headcount from the org chart, a lead time from the supply agreement. Blocks with nothing
behind them are still allowed — but they are marked as assumptions and stay marked, rather
than being quietly promoted to facts by the passage of time.

**The contradictions stay visible.** When the value proposition promises a response time the
key resources cannot deliver, the canvas records both and flags the disagreement. Resolving
it is a decision, and decisions belong to people. Hiding it is a lie, and lies belong
nowhere.

**It is typed, not prose.** Segments are a list of segments with sizes and revenue shares,
not a paragraph about "SMB and mid-market". That distinction is what lets the next framework
read this one: Five Forces wants the concentration figure, VRIO wants the resource list, the
conductor wants the activities. Frameworks pass fields to each other. Paragraphs stop at
the edge of the page.

**It has an owner and a date.** Both of which are the same discipline as the first three,
applied to the canvas itself: a model that cannot say how old it is cannot be trusted with
what it says.

Run this well and you have not answered a single strategic question. You have made the rest
of them answerable — which is the only thing a map is ever for.
