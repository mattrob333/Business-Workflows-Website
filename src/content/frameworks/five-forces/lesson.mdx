## What it is

Five Forces is a question about structure, not about competitors. It asks where the profit
in a market ends up, and it answers by naming the five parties that can take it: the rivals
already in the market, the buyers, the suppliers, the substitutes that solve the same problem
another way, and the entrants who are not here yet.

Score each force on how much pressure it puts on the firm, and a shape appears. A market where
buyers are concentrated, suppliers are few and switching is easy is a market that will be hard
to earn a margin in no matter how well the business is run. A market where the reverse is true
forgives a great deal of ordinary management.

That is the uncomfortable half of the framework, and the reason it is worth running. It says
some of your margin is a property of the terrain rather than of your effort — and it tells you
which part.

## When to run it, and when not

Run it after the canvas, because the forces are scored against a segment and the canvas is
where the segments got written down. Run it when a pricing decision, a market-entry question or
an acquisition is on the table, and run it again whenever the terrain moves: a supplier
consolidates, a large customer merges, a certification requirement disappears.

Score one market at a time. "Commercial HVAC" is not a market; multi-tenant property management
and public-sector procurement behave so differently that averaging them produces a picture of
neither.

Do not run it on your own company. The unit of analysis is the market, and a firm that scores
the forces on itself ends up describing its own performance in the vocabulary of structure,
which is the most flattering mistake available. Do not run it to rank competitors — rivalry is
one of five forces and the others are usually louder. And do not run it to decide what to fix
this quarter. It tells you which pressure sets the ceiling on margin. It has nothing at all to
say about which stage of your own operation sets the ceiling on volume, and confusing the two
is how a business spends a year negotiating supplier terms while the work piles up somewhere
else entirely.

## How it became wallpaper

This one did not die on a poster. It died as an essay.

The industry analysis is written once, for a funding round or a strategy offsite. Five headings,
five paragraphs, a sentence of hedging under each. It reads well, because prose about markets
always reads well. Then it goes into an appendix, and it is never opened again — not because
anybody decided it was finished, but because there is nothing in it that could be re-run.

That is the failure, and it is structural rather than lazy. A paragraph cannot be compared to
last year's paragraph. It has no score to move, no evidence to refresh, no date that visibly
expires. So when the distributor's lead times double, nothing in the document changes, and the
sentence "supplier power is moderate" sits there being wrong in a font.

The tell is always the same: ask what the analysis would look like if one force got materially
worse, and the honest answer is that it would look identical, because it was written to be read
rather than to be run.

> An industry analysis you cannot re-run is not an analysis. It is an essay about a year that has already ended.

## What good looks like

**Every force is a score with something underneath it.** Buyer power is not "high"; it is a
number attached to a concentration figure from the accounting system, a switching-cost
observation from a contract, and a lost deal with a recorded reason. The score is what makes it
comparable to next quarter's. The evidence is what makes it worth comparing.

**Scores are assumptions until the evidence promotes them.** Most first-pass force scores are
opinions in a numeric costume, and marking them as such is not weakness — it is what tells you
which one to go and check. A force nobody can source is an open question, and open questions
are an output of this framework too.

**One force gets named as the primary pressure.** Five scores with no ranking is a scorecard,
not a finding. The model has to commit to which pressure most determines profitability in this
market right now, and say what would have to change for the answer to be different.

**The sensitivity is tested, not asserted.** Move a score and see whether the picture reorders.
If the ranking survives a two-point swing in any single force, the finding is robust. If it
flips, the analysis is really a statement about one uncertain number, and it should say so.

**The strongest force is not the instruction.** A market can have real buyer power and still be
limited by something the buyers have nothing to do with. Five Forces sets the ceiling on price.
Something else usually sets the ceiling on volume — and the whole point of running these
frameworks as one system is that you get to find out which is which.
