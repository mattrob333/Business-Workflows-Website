## What it is

Every system that turns demand into delivered work has a rate, and that rate is set in one
place. Theory of Constraints is the method for finding the place: identify the constraint,
exploit it, subordinate everything else to it, elevate it, and when it moves — because it
always moves — start again.

The claim underneath is arithmetic rather than philosophy. The throughput of a chain of
stages is the capacity of its narrowest stage. Not the average. Not the sum of the
improvements you made this quarter. The narrowest one. Which means an hour gained anywhere
else in the system is worth nothing at all, and an hour gained at the constraint is worth an
hour of the whole business.

That is an uncomfortable claim, because it says most improvement work is decorative. It is
also testable, which is what separates this framework from an opinion about focus.

## When to run it, and when not

Run it when a business is busy and not growing — when the effort curve and the output curve
have visibly come apart. Rising revenue on flat delivery, longer queues on better win rates,
more people arriving with no more work leaving. Run it once you have some description of the
flow and some measure of capacity per stage, because the method needs both and will invent
neither.

Do not run it before there is throughput to protect. A company still looking for its first
customers has a constraint, but it is knowledge, and pointing a pipe diagram at it produces
a confident answer about the wrong system. Do not run it to ratify a decision someone has
already made; the constraint is exactly the thing that does not care what you would prefer
it to be. And do not run it on a schedule. Constraints move when something changes, not when
the month does — and hunting one every month is how a company ends up improving five stages
by ten per cent each and shipping the same amount it shipped last year.

## How it became wallpaper

This one did not die on a poster. It died as an anecdote.

A generation of managers read the novel, kept the word *bottleneck*, and dropped the
measurement. What survives in most organisations is the vocabulary without the method: teams
name their constraint in a meeting, by nomination. Whoever is loudest gets nominated.
Whichever department looks most visibly stressed gets nominated. Sometimes whoever is
absent gets nominated.

The nomination is usually wrong, and it is wrong in a predictable direction. The stage that
complains most is rarely the stage with the least capacity — complaint tracks pressure, and
pressure is highest just *upstream* of the constraint, where work arrives faster than it can
leave. So the improvement lands next to the bottleneck rather than on it. A local metric
moves. The system's output does not. And then the framework gets blamed for a diagnosis
nobody actually performed.

> Improving a stage that was never the limit is not a small win. It is a cost with a dashboard.

## What good looks like

**Capacity is measured in one unit, per stage.** Jobs per week, projects per year, tickets
per day — whatever the system delivers, expressed the same way at every stage. Stages that
cannot be measured in that unit are not stages, they are activities, and they belong in a
different diagram.

**The constraint is computed, not chosen.** It is whichever stage has the least capacity.
Not the one with the worst mood, the newest manager or the oldest software. If the numbers
say the constraint is somewhere unfashionable, the numbers are the finding.

**The alternatives are written down and rejected in public.** Every real business has four
or five true problems competing for the title. Concentration risk is real. Supplier lead
times are real. The single approver is real. A good diagnosis names each one, shows the
evidence that keeps it from being binding, and leaves the list on the page — so the person
who asks "but what about pricing?" in six weeks gets an answer instead of a re-run.

**Exploit comes before elevate.** The instinct is to buy more of the constrained thing.
That is step four, it is the slowest step, and it is frequently unnecessary: an hour the
constraint spends on work it should not be doing is an hour of the entire system's output,
recoverable this week for nothing.

**The next constraint is named in advance.** Widen a stage and the rate moves somewhere else
by definition. A diagnosis that does not say where it will move next is not finished — it is
just this quarter's answer, waiting to become next quarter's surprise.
