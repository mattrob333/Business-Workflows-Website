## What it is

Ansoff sorts every growth idea by two questions: is the product one we already have, and is
the market one we already serve? Four quadrants follow — penetration, market development,
product development, diversification — and they are ordered by how much the business has to
learn before the move can work.

The matrix is not a menu. Read as boxes it is trivial and slightly insulting; read as a map
of *routes* it is useful, because the interesting thing about a growth plan is rarely the
destination and almost always the number of unfamiliar steps between here and there. Selling
what you have to people you already understand asks nothing new of the organisation. Selling
something new to someone new asks for a second company.

So the real output is not "we chose diversification". It is: the option, the capabilities it
requires, the ones the business demonstrably has, and the assumptions standing in for the
rest.

## When it earns its place

Run it after VRIO and after the terrain read. Ansoff's quadrants are only honest when the
inputs are honest about what the business can actually do — otherwise every route looks
equally available and the framework degrades into a preference poll.

Run it before Three Horizons, which needs a set of candidate options before it can ask
whether the portfolio is balanced.

> Every step away from what you know is a step you will pay for in learning, and the matrix
> exists to make you count the steps.

Do not use it to justify a decision already made. The quadrant a proposal lands in is
determined by facts about the product and the market, not by how the proposal was described
— and a team that has decided will describe a new market as an adjacent one without noticing
it is doing so. Write down who the buyer is and what they currently do instead. If nobody
in the room has met one, the market is new.

What good looks like: relative risk stated per option rather than ranked in the abstract,
required capabilities named against the VRIO output, and the diversification quadrant
either populated with something real or left visibly empty. A matrix with an idea in every
box usually means the boxes were filled to be filled.
